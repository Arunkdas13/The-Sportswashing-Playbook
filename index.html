Khaldoon Al Mubarak — Manchester City

Abu Dhabi | Premier League

The friction arrived in 2018 when German magazine Der Spiegel published investigative reports based on emails hacked by Portuguese whistleblower Rui Pinto, known as Football Leaks. The leaked documents alleged that Abu Dhabi owner Sheikh Mansour had routed funds through UAE-based sponsor companies — including Etihad Airways and Aabar Investment — to funnel money into Manchester City while disguising it as commercial sponsorship income. Those accounts, the documents alleged, were managed by Al Mubarak's own government agency, the Executive Affairs Authority ("Khaldoon Khalifa" — Wikipedia).

"UEFA investigated and initially banned Manchester City from European competition for two seasons. The Court of Arbitration for Sport overturned it — not because City was cleared, but because the statute of limitations had run on some charges."

The Premier League launched its own separate investigation, which ran for four years before producing formal charges in February 2023.

Yasir Al-Rumayyan — Newcastle United & LIV Golf

Saudi Arabia | Premier League & Golf

For Al-Rumayyan, the friction came from two directions simultaneously. The PGA Tour, facing mass defections of its top players to LIV, filed an antitrust lawsuit. In their legal response, LIV's lawyers argued that because PIF was a sovereign government fund, it was entitled to sovereign immunity and could not be compelled to produce documents in US proceedings.

"They described PIF as 'a sovereign instrumentality of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia' and Al-Rumayyan as 'a sitting minister of the Saudi government' — the exact opposite of what PIF had told the Premier League." ("Court Filing Casts")

Amnesty International immediately called on the Premier League to re-examine the assurances it had accepted, with its UK Economic Affairs Director stating it was "stretching credulity to breaking point" to believe the Saudi state was not directing the buyout ("Premier League Told").

Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani — The 2022 World Cup

Qatar | FIFA

For Qatar, the friction was visible from the moment the bid was won in 2010. Qatar's Kafala system — under which a worker's ability to remain in Qatar legally depended entirely on their employer's approval, leaving them unable to switch jobs or return home without that sponsor's consent — was documented extensively by Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, and The Guardian before a single stadium had been built. Qatar announced plans to abolish Kafala in 2014 but delayed implementation until 2016, and even the eventual reforms still left employers with significant control over workers ("Human Rights Issues"). The International Trade Union Confederation estimated as early as 2013 that over 1,200 workers had died on infrastructure projects connected to the World Cup. A 2021 Guardian investigation put the figure at 6,500 migrant worker deaths since the bid was awarded.

Phase 3 — The Dirt

2022 — Present

No more game framing. Just what happened.

Red Card

Manchester City — 115 Charges

Still unresolved. Still playing. Still spending.

Manchester City's 115 charges remain unresolved. The Premier League formally charged the club in February 2023 with more than 100 alleged breaches of financial rules covering a nine-year period from 2009 to 2018 — with the true number of charges believed to be closer to 130 ("Man City Premier League"). An independent commission heard the case across ten weeks from September to December 2024. As of early 2026, no verdict has been delivered. Since the hearing concluded, Manchester City spent nearly £500 million on new players ("Man City 115 Charges"). The delay became its own scandal, with Premier League officials privately expressing it was causing "undue damage" to the competition's credibility ("Man City FFP Case").

Red Card

Nasser Al-Khelaifi — Criminal Investigation

Vote buying. Broadcasting rights. Power.

Al-Khelaifi was placed under formal criminal investigation in France on February 5, 2025, charged with complicity in vote buying, interference with voting freedom, and abuse of power in connection with the SCA Lagardere case ("Nasser Al-Khelaifi's Indictment"). This followed earlier Swiss federal charges in 2020 connected to World Cup broadcasting rights, which were ultimately dismissed. French prosecutors were also examining two payments totaling $3.5 million made by a company run by his brother, around the time Qatar was bidding to host major international athletics events ("PSG Chief Charged"). Qatar reportedly considered withdrawing its investment from both PSG and beIN Sports — a move that would devastate French football's broadcasting infrastructure entirely.

Red Card

Qatar World Cup — The Human Cost

The most serious consequence. The least resolved.

Qatar's own World Cup secretary-general admitted in a November 2022 television interview that between 400 and 500 migrant workers had died in connection with tournament preparations, while simultaneously repeating the official position that only three work-related deaths had occurred on stadium construction sites — a direct contradiction that Human Rights Watch called a striking inadvertent admission ("Qatar World Cup Chief"). A FIFA-commissioned report released in 2024 found that "severe human rights impacts did ultimately occur in Qatar from 2010 through 2022" and that "a credible argument can be made" that FIFA bore some responsibility — even as FIFA simultaneously declared Saudi Arabia's unopposed bid for the 2034 World Cup a "very strong all-round proposition" (Minder). No compensation fund for deceased workers' families was ever established. FIFA did not mandate one.

Red Card

Formula 1 — The Dependency Exposed

Bahrain and Saudi races cancelled. March 2026.

Formula 1's Gulf dependency was exposed in March 2026 when the Bahrain and Saudi Arabian Grands Prix were cancelled due to regional conflict following US-Israeli strikes and Iranian retaliation. F1 CEO Stefano Domenicali described both countries as "incredibly important to the ecosystem of our racing season" while announcing their cancellation ("Bahrain and Saudi Arabian"). Bahrain and Saudi Arabia alone accounted for an estimated $115 million in combined annual hosting fees ("What the Bahrain"). The cancellations revealed what critics had argued for years: a sport that structured a third of its calendar around nations paying from state treasuries had made itself financially dependent on governments operating under entirely different rules than private commercial promoters.

The Common Source
Oil revenue managed by governments with documented human rights concerns — deployed through sports as the primary vehicle for international rebranding

The financial architecture behind every character in the simulator connects to a sovereign wealth fund. Mubadala controls Manchester City. The Qatar Investment Authority controls PSG and bankrolled the World Cup. The Saudi Public Investment Fund controls Newcastle, LIV Golf, and now has an exclusive agreement to invest further into the new PGA-LIV combined entity. All three funds operate from the same source — oil revenue managed by governments with documented human rights concerns — and all three deployed sports as the primary vehicle for international rebranding.

Scholars at Columbia University's Journal of International Affairs describe Qatar's approach as exercising "sharp power" — a form of influence that goes beyond soft power by actively shaping the institutions through which global sports are governed ("The Authoritarian's"). The same framework applies across all three nations. By the time FIFA, UEFA, the Premier League, the PGA Tour, and Formula 1 understood the scale of what was happening, every one of those institutions had already taken the money. The central question The Playbook poses: if every governing body was financially dependent on Gulf investment, who was left to blow the whistle?

The Verdict

You have seen the decisions.
You have seen the consequences.
You have seen who looked away.

UEFA settled with Manchester City rather than pursuing expulsion. The Premier League accepted assurances about Saudi state involvement that were later contradicted in a US federal court. FIFA awarded Qatar the World Cup despite documented labor rights abuses, then commissioned a report twelve years later acknowledging its own responsibility — while awarding the next World Cup to Saudi Arabia. The PGA Tour filed an antitrust lawsuit against LIV Golf and then attempted to merge with it. Formula 1 built a third of its calendar around nations paying hosting fees from state treasuries, then described those nations as essential to its season when conflict made them temporarily unreachable.

None of this was secret. The leaked emails, the court filings, the Guardian's investigation, the FIFA documents, the Amnesty International letters — all of it was public. The heist succeeded not because it was hidden, but because every institution with the power to stop it decided it was more profitable to look away.
